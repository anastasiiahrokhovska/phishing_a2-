IT ENGLISH · LESSON PLAN

🛡️ Hack or Not? Staying Safe Online

A 60-minute interactive lesson on cybersecurity basics for IT learners.

Level: A2+60 minPairs & groupsSpeaking · Reading · Grammar

Lesson aims

  • Learn 10 key security words (phishing, password, malware…).
  • Read a short text about a scam email and find red flags.
  • Give advice with should / shouldn’t / must / Don’t…
  • Role-play a call to an IT helpdesk.

Materials: screen or printed email, 10 word cards (optional), a timer. Click a stage below to jump to it.

2. Vocabulary: Security toolbox

5–13 min

Present and practise 10 words. Students first try to guess the meaning, then check.

hackera person who enters a system without permission
phishinga fake message that steals your data
malwarebad software (virus, spyware)
passworda secret word to log in
two-factor authentication (2FA)a second step to log in, e.g. an SMS code
firewalla wall that blocks dangerous traffic
updatea new version that fixes problems
backupa safe copy of your files
suspiciousstrange; you don’t trust it
scama trick to get your money

Activity: Quick gap-fill (individually, then check in pairs)

  1. I got an email from “my bank” with a strange link. It’s ….
  2. My laptop is slow and I see pop-ups. Maybe it has ….
  3. I always make a … of my photos on the cloud.
  4. To log in, I need my password and a code from my phone. That’s ….
  5. Please install the latest … for Windows.
Answers1 suspicious / phishing · 2 malware · 3 backup · 4 two-factor authentication · 5 update

Task 1: Complete the sentences. Word bank: trust · urgent · link · @ · report

  2. Scammers want you to click a … or open an attachment.
  3. Phishing messages often ask you to do something ….

Task 2: Put the steps in order (how to check a message, as in the video)

  1. Hover over the link to see the full web address.
  2. If it still looks strange, contact the company directly.
  3. Check who sent the email (look after the @).
  4. Ask: Is the message urgent? Is it about something in my life right now?
Suggested orderd → c → a → b. Then report the message if it looks like a scam.

After watching (6 min)

  • Which tip is the most useful for you? Why?
  • Compare the video with Olena’s story (next stage): what is the same?
  • In pairs, explain phishing to a friend who isn’t good with computers: “Phishing is when …”

Teacher’s tip: if the video doesn’t load, read the backup script below aloud twice (slowly) and do the same tasks.

Backup script (read aloud)

Scammers send messages that look like they are from your bank, a courier or your boss. They want you to click a link or open a file. First, ask: is the message urgent? Then check the sender’s address. The part after the @ sign must match the real company. Hover over the link and read the full web address. If something looks strange, don’t click. Contact the company directly and report the message.

4. Reading: “The email from Netflix”

25–35 min

Gist reading → detail → discussion.

Pre-reading (2 min): Look at the title. Why can an email from Netflix be dangerous?

Olena is a junior QA engineer. Last Friday, she got an email: “Your Netflix account is blocked. Click the link and enter your card number in 24 hours.”

She was tired, so she almost clicked. But then she looked at the sender: support@netfIix-help.com. The word “Netflix” had a capital “I” instead of the letter “l”. Also, the email said “Dear customer”, not her name.

Olena didn’t click the link. She sent the email to the security team and deleted it. Her colleague Max wasn’t so lucky. He clicked a similar link last month, and hackers stole his work password. The company had to change all its passwords.

Task A (gist, 2 min): What’s the best title? a) A free movie b) A lucky escape c) A new job

Task B: True or False? (click to check)

Task C (discussion, 3 min): Have you ever got a suspicious message? What did you do?

AnswersA: b · 1 T · 2 F (she saw the fake sender) · 3 T · 4 F (she sent it to security) · 5 T

Task D: Find a word in the text that means:

  1. the person who sends an email
  2. nearly
  3. took something illegally
  4. almost the same
Answers1 sender · 2 almost · 3 stole · 4 similar

5. Grammar: Giving safety advice

35–44 min

Use should / shouldn’t / must / imperatives naturally.

StructureMeaningExample
You should + verbgood adviceYou should use 2FA.
You shouldn’t + verbbad ideaYou shouldn’t share your password.
You must + verbstrong ruleYou must update your system.
Don’t + verbdirect warningDon’t click unknown links!

Practice 1: Choose the correct word

  1. You (should / shouldn’t) use the same password everywhere.
  2. Employees (must / mustn’t) report phishing emails.
  3. (Do / Don’t) open files from strangers.
  4. You (should / shouldn’t) make a backup every week.

Practice 2: Make it yours. Write 3 pieces of advice for your grandma who just bought a smartphone. Compare with a partner.

Answers1 shouldn’t · 2 must · 3 Don’t · 4 should

Practice 3: Find and correct the mistake

  1. You should to use two-factor authentication.
  2. Don’t to click unknown links.
  3. You mustn’t sharing your password.
  4. She shoulds make a backup.
Answers1 You should use … · 2 Don’t click … · 3 You mustn’t share … · 4 She should make …

Quick reminder: mustn’t vs shouldn’t

should / shouldn’t = advice (a good or bad idea). must = a strong rule or something necessary. mustn’t = it is forbidden / do not do it. Don’t + verb = a direct instruction or warning.

After these modal verbs, use the base form: must change, not must to change or must changing.

Practice 12: Grammar challenge — the security policy

Write a short security policy for a small IT company. Include at least 8 rules:

  • 2 sentences with must
  • 2 sentences with mustn’t
  • 2 sentences with should or shouldn’t
  • 2 sentences with Don’t

Try to use these words: password, backup, update, suspicious link, personal data, attachment, account, Wi-Fi.

Extension: swap policies with a partner. Check the modal verb, the verb form after it, and whether each rule makes sense.

6. Game: Phishing detective 🔍

44–51 min

Apply vocabulary in a fun, collaborative task. Teams of 2–3, 4 minutes to find as many red flags as possible.

From: security@paypa1-center.net
To: Dear user
Subject: URGENT!!! Your account will be deleted

Hello,
We noticed a problem with you account. Click here now: http://bit.ly/x9f-secure
Send us your password and card number in 2 hours or we will close it.
Open the attached file: invoice.exe

Best regards,
Support Team

Teams report: “I think this is suspicious because …” — the team with most correct flags wins.

Red flags (8+)
  • Fake domain (paypa1 with the number 1)
  • “Dear user”, no name
  • Urgent tone and threat
  • Spelling mistake (“with you account”)
  • Short, unknown link
  • Asks for password and card number
  • Very short deadline
  • Dangerous attachment (.exe)

7. Role-play: IT helpdesk call 📞

51–57 min

Free speaking practice with a real-life workplace situation. Swap roles after 4 minutes.

Student A: Employee. You clicked a suspicious link 10 minutes ago. Your screen shows strange pop-ups. You’re worried.
Student B: Helpdesk. Ask questions. Give advice.

Useful language

  • Helpdesk: What happened? · When did you click the link? · Did you enter your password? · Please don’t turn off the computer. · You should change your password now.
  • Employee: I clicked a link in an email. · I think it’s malware. · What should I do? · Can you help me, please?

Monitor, note 3–4 good sentences and 3 common mistakes for feedback at the end.

8. Wrap-up: Exit ticket

57–60 min

Each student completes one sentence aloud or in the chat:

  • Today I learned the word …
  • From now on, I will … / I shouldn’t …

Praise 2–3 good answers. Give homework.

🏠 Homework

  1. Write 5 rules for a safe password (should / shouldn’t / must).
  2. Find one real phishing example online and write 3 red flags in English.
  3. Bonus: a short voice message (30 sec): “How do I protect my phone?”

Teacher’s notes: If time is short, shorten stage 6 to one round. For stronger groups, add a discussion: “Is a password manager a good idea?” For weaker groups, give the word list with translations during the reading.
